Harbor Council Approves Revised Flood Barrier Plan

BRANDEMERE — The Brandemere Harbor Council voted 7 to 2 on Tuesday evening to approve a revised plan for a movable flood barrier across the mouth of the Lenna estuary, ending nearly three years of argument over cost, routing and the effect on shipping. The barrier, which engineers say could be completed in 2031, will consist of four steel gates that rest on the seabed during normal conditions and rise when forecast water levels pass a set threshold.

Council chair Imelda Roarke said the decision reflected "a willingness on all sides to accept a plan that nobody likes entirely and everybody can live with." The estimated construction budget stands at 412 million marks, of which the national water agency has promised 55 percent. The remainder will be raised through a bond issue and a small surcharge on harbor fees, which the council expects to average 1.8 percent per vessel call.

Opponents on the council argued that the money would be better spent on raising low-lying streets and restoring salt marshes upstream. Councillor Tobias Venkataraman, one of the two dissenting votes, said that the barrier "protects the harbor, not the neighborhoods behind it." Supporters replied that a layered approach was still possible, and that the plan includes a fund of 30 million marks for marsh restoration and drainage upgrades in the eastern districts.

Fishing cooperatives, which had objected to an earlier design with a fixed sill, welcomed the change. A spokesperson for the Lenna Fishers' Association said the new gates would leave a navigable channel 140 meters wide open at all but the most severe tides. Construction is scheduled to start in the spring after an environmental review, which will examine sediment movement and the migration of two protected fish species.

How Does a Heat Pump Work?

A heat pump moves heat from one place to another instead of generating it by burning fuel or running electricity through a resistive element. The idea is the same as in a refrigerator, but used in the other direction for heating a home. A closed loop contains a refrigerant, a fluid that boils at a very low temperature. In the outdoor unit, the refrigerant is a cold, low-pressure liquid. Even on a cool day the outdoor air is warmer than the refrigerant, so heat flows into the liquid and makes it evaporate.

The gas then passes through a compressor. Compressing a gas raises both its pressure and its temperature, so the refrigerant leaves the compressor hot, often above 60 degrees Celsius. This hot gas flows through a coil inside the house, where a fan blows room air across it or where it warms water for radiators. As the refrigerant gives up its heat, it condenses back into a liquid. Finally an expansion valve drops the pressure, the liquid cools sharply, and the cycle begins again.

The key figure for comparing machines is the coefficient of performance, abbreviated COP. A COP of 3.5 means that for each kilowatt-hour of electricity used to run the compressor and fans, 3.5 kilowatt-hours of heat are delivered. Performance falls as the temperature difference between outside and inside grows, which is why installers size the system for the coldest expected days and why a well insulated house matters at least as much as the machine itself.

How to Replace a Leaking Kitchen Faucet Cartridge

Before you begin, gather an adjustable wrench, a set of hex keys, a small flat screwdriver, a replacement cartridge that matches your faucet model, a towel and some plumber's grease. Allow about forty minutes.

1. Turn off both shutoff valves under the sink. If you cannot find them, close the main supply to the house instead. Open the faucet to release any remaining pressure and drain the lines.
2. Place the towel in the bottom of the sink so that small parts do not fall down the drain.
3. Pry off the decorative cap on top of the handle with the flat screwdriver. Underneath you will find a screw. Remove it and lift the handle straight off. If it is stuck, wiggle it gently; do not force it.
4. Unscrew the retaining nut with the wrench. Some models use a clip instead of a nut. Take a photograph before removing anything so you can remember the orientation.
5. Pull the old cartridge straight up. Note the position of the notch or tab on its side, because the new one must sit in exactly the same way.
6. Wipe the inside of the faucet body with a damp cloth to remove mineral deposits. Apply a thin film of plumber's grease to the rubber rings of the new cartridge.
7. Push the new cartridge in until it seats fully, replace the nut, handle and screw, and then reopen the shutoff valves slowly. Run hot and cold water for a minute and check underneath for drips.

If the leak continues after replacement, the valve seat inside the body may be worn, and the whole faucet will probably need to be replaced.

The Lighthouse Keeper's Daughter

Wren had lived on the rock for all of her eleven years, and in that time she had learned that the sea had moods the way people did. There was a sulking gray, a bright and flirtatious blue, and a dark green that meant her father would not sleep that night. On the evening the supply boat failed to come, the sea was a color she had never seen: pale, flat, like milk spilled on a slate.

"Something is coming," her father said, and went up the spiral stairs two steps at a time to polish the lens, though he had polished it that morning. Wren followed with a cup of tea she knew he would forget to drink. From the lamp room she could see the whole horizon, and for a long while there was nothing on it but the faint line where water became sky.

Then a smudge appeared, very small, and another beside it. Her father lifted the brass telescope, said nothing, and handed it to her. She saw a flotilla of tiny boats, a dozen at least, rowing hard against the swell. In the lead boat a woman stood and waved a lantern in wide, desperate arcs.

"Light the lamp," Wren whispered, although the lamp was already lit. Her father shook his head and pointed instead to the old signal horn that hung unused beside the window. "They cannot see the rocks in this haze. They will need to hear us as well." So they sounded it, three long blasts and one short, over and over, until Wren's arms ached and the first boat scraped onto the shingle below. It was past midnight before the last villager from the drowned island climbed the steps, wet and shivering, into the warm kitchen where Wren had put every pot of water on the stove.

The History of the Bicycle Chain

Early velocipedes had pedals attached directly to the front wheel, which meant that the speed of the bicycle was limited by how fast a rider could turn their legs. Makers responded by building ever larger front wheels, which gave rise to the high-wheeler, a machine that was fast but dangerous to ride. The invention of the chain drive in the late nineteenth century changed everything. With a chain connecting the pedals to the rear wheel, gearing could be chosen independently of wheel size, and designers could make both wheels equal and low. The result, the so-called safety bicycle, is recognizably the ancestor of the bicycles we ride today. Roller chains, which use small cylinders to reduce friction between links and sprocket teeth, soon became standard, and their basic design has remained almost unchanged for over a century.

Why Software Estimates Slip

Almost every team has had the experience of estimating a task at three days and finishing it in nine. The usual explanation is optimism, but optimism is only part of the story. When we estimate, we picture the main path: the code we will write, the tests we will add, the review we will request. We do not picture the unrelated outage that pulls two people away for an afternoon, the dependency that changes its interface, or the requirement that turns out to have been misunderstood. These events are individually unlikely and collectively almost certain, so the true duration of a task is better described as a distribution with a long right tail than as a single number.

One practical response is to estimate ranges rather than points, and to keep a record of how past estimates compared with reality. A team that sees, over twenty tasks, that its median overrun is forty percent can simply multiply its next estimate by 1.4 and be right more often than not. Another response is to cut work into pieces small enough that each fits in a day or two; small tasks have narrower tails, and the sum of many narrow tails behaves more predictably than one wide one. Neither method removes uncertainty, but both make it visible, and visible uncertainty can be managed.

Notes on Reading a Nutrition Label

The first thing to check is the serving size, because every other number on the label refers to it. A bag of crackers may list 130 calories per serving, but if the bag contains five servings, eating it all means roughly 650 calories. Next, look at added sugars, which are listed separately from naturally occurring sugars in many countries. A drink with 28 grams of added sugar contains about seven teaspoons. Sodium is the third item worth a glance: adults are generally advised to keep intake under about 2,300 milligrams per day, and a single canned soup can supply a third of that. Finally, read the ingredient list; ingredients are ordered by weight, so the first three tell you most of what the product is.

Short Story: The Clockmaker's Apprentice

Odalys had been told not to touch the grandfather clock in the back room, which is precisely why she was standing in front of it at midnight with a lamp in one hand and a thin screwdriver in the other. The clock had not run in forty years. Its pendulum hung still, its face the color of old teeth, and yet some evenings, when the shop was quiet, she was certain she heard a faint tick.

She opened the case. Inside, behind the weights, lay a folded piece of paper wedged in the gears. Carefully, holding her breath, she drew it out. It was a hand-drawn map of the town, but a town she did not recognize: the river bent the wrong way, the market square was a park, and in the corner someone had written, in a neat, old-fashioned hand, "What was, and what may yet be."

She was still staring at it when the pendulum swung once, twice, and the tick filled the room. Outside the window the streetlights flickered, and for one dizzying moment the road beyond the glass was a field of tall grass under a sky full of unfamiliar stars. Then it was gone, the clock was silent, and the paper in her hand had turned blank. Odalys closed the case, replaced the lamp, and said nothing to anyone. But the next morning she began, quietly, to learn how to repair clocks properly.

A Brief Guide to Composting at Home

Composting turns kitchen and garden waste into a dark, crumbly soil improver. The method relies on balancing two kinds of material. Greens, such as vegetable peelings, coffee grounds and grass clippings, are rich in nitrogen. Browns, such as dry leaves, cardboard and straw, are rich in carbon. A good rule of thumb is about two parts brown to one part green by volume. Too much green produces a wet, smelly heap; too much brown makes it slow.

Keep the pile moist like a wrung-out sponge and turn it every week or two to add air. In warm weather, a well-managed heap can be ready in two to three months; in cold weather expect six or more. Avoid meat, dairy and oily food, which attract pests, and avoid weeds that have gone to seed. When the material looks like dark soil and smells earthy, it is ready to spread around plants.

Introduction to Binary Search

Binary search finds a target value in a sorted array by repeatedly halving the range that could contain it. Compare the target with the middle element. If they are equal, you are done. If the target is smaller, discard the upper half; if larger, discard the lower half. Because each step halves the remaining range, an array of one million elements needs at most twenty comparisons, since 2^20 is slightly more than a million. The running time is therefore logarithmic, written O(log n). The method only works when the data is sorted, and a common source of bugs is computing the midpoint as (low + high) / 2, which can overflow for very large indices in languages with fixed-size integers; writing low + (high - low) / 2 avoids the problem.

Bước 1: Làm sạch cá, cắt khúc vừa ăn, ướp với một thìa cà phê muối và chút tiêu trong mười lăm phút.
Bước 2: Ngâm me với nước nóng, bóp nhẹ rồi lọc lấy nước cốt. Cà chua cắt múi cau, dứa cắt miếng mỏng, đậu bắp cắt xéo, bạc hà tước vỏ rồi thái lát.
Bước 3: Phi thơm tỏi băm trong nồi, cho cà chua vào xào sơ. Đổ khoảng một lít rưỡi nước và nước me vào, đun sôi.
Bước 4: Cho cá vào, hạ lửa nhỏ nấu khoảng tám phút. Thêm dứa, bạc hà, đậu bắp. Nêm nước mắm, đường cho vừa miệng; vị canh phải chua thanh, hơi ngọt.
Bước 5: Tắt bếp, cho giá đỗ, ngò gai, hành lá vào rồi múc ra tô. Ăn nóng với cơm trắng và nước mắm ớt.

Cậu bé và chiếc diều giấy

Ngày xưa, ở một làng nhỏ bên bờ sông, có cậu bé tên Tí rất thích thả diều. Mỗi chiều, sau khi giúp mẹ cho gà ăn, cậu lại chạy ra bãi cỏ cuối làng, nơi gió từ sông thổi lên mát rượi. Chiếc diều của cậu làm bằng giấy báo cũ, khung tre vót mảnh, đuôi dài kết từ những mảnh vải vụn.

Một hôm, gió nổi mạnh bất ngờ. Dây diều căng lên, kéo Tí chạy theo mấy bước rồi bứt khỏi tay cậu. Chiếc diều vút lên cao, lượn qua mái đình, qua cánh đồng, rồi mất hút sau rặng tre. Tí ngồi bệt xuống đất, nước mắt chực trào. Bà cụ hàng xóm đi ngang, nhìn cậu và mỉm cười: "Diều bay đi là để có chỗ cho cái diều đẹp hơn con sẽ làm ngày mai."

Tối đó, Tí ngồi dưới ngọn đèn dầu, cẩn thận cắt giấy, vót tre. Lần này cậu dán thêm một ngôi sao nhỏ ở giữa cánh. Mấy hôm sau, một đứa trẻ làng bên mang đến trả chiếc diều cũ, mắc trên cây gạo, rách một góc. Hai đứa cùng vá lại, và từ đó chiều nào chúng cũng thả diều bên nhau.

Giải thích về bộ nhớ GPU và vì sao mô hình ngôn ngữ cần nhiều VRAM

Khi chạy một mô hình ngôn ngữ lớn trên card đồ họa, phần lớn bộ nhớ video (VRAM) được dùng để chứa trọng số của mô hình. Ví dụ, một mô hình có 7 tỷ tham số lưu ở độ chính xác 16 bit cần khoảng 14 GB chỉ cho trọng số. Nếu lượng tử hóa xuống 4 bit, con số này giảm còn khoảng 4 đến 5 GB, đổi lại chất lượng có thể giảm đôi chút. Ngoài trọng số, bộ nhớ còn phải chứa bộ đệm khóa và giá trị (KV cache), tăng tuyến tính theo độ dài ngữ cảnh và số lượng yêu cầu chạy đồng thời.

Khi một card không đủ bộ nhớ, ta có thể chia mô hình ra nhiều GPU. Có hai cách phổ biến. Cách thứ nhất là chia theo lớp: mỗi card giữ một nhóm lớp liên tiếp, dữ liệu đi qua từng card lần lượt. Cách này ít tốn băng thông giữa các card nhưng các card phải chờ nhau. Cách thứ hai là chia từng ma trận (tensor parallel), cho phép các card tính đồng thời nhưng đòi hỏi đường truyền nhanh như NVLink. Trên máy tính cá nhân chỉ có PCIe, chia theo lớp thường là lựa chọn thực tế hơn.

Một điểm cần lưu ý là tốc độ sinh token thường bị giới hạn bởi băng thông bộ nhớ chứ không phải sức mạnh tính toán. Mỗi token mới đòi hỏi đọc lại gần như toàn bộ trọng số từ VRAM, vì vậy card có băng thông 900 GB/s sẽ nhanh hơn rõ rệt so với card 300 GB/s dù số nhân tính toán tương đương.

Hướng dẫn cài đặt trình điều khiển cho card đồ họa

1. Kiểm tra tên card bằng lệnh nvidia-smi trong cửa sổ dòng lệnh. Nếu lệnh báo không tìm thấy, bạn cần cài trình điều khiển trước.
2. Tải bản trình điều khiển phù hợp với hệ điều hành từ trang chủ của nhà sản xuất, và kiểm tra phiên bản CUDA tương ứng.
3. Đóng các ứng dụng đang dùng GPU, chạy trình cài đặt và chọn cài đặt tùy chỉnh nếu muốn cài sạch.
4. Khởi động lại máy, rồi chạy lại nvidia-smi để xác nhận. Bạn sẽ thấy tên card, dung lượng bộ nhớ và nhiệt độ hiện tại.
5. Nếu máy có hai card, hãy chắc chắn nguồn điện đủ công suất; tổng điện năng tiêu thụ của hai card cao cấp có thể vượt 700 W.

Bạn: Máy mình chạy mô hình rất chậm, nhiệt độ GPU lên tới 87 độ. Có sao không?
Trợ lý: Nhiệt độ 87 độ C nằm ở ngưỡng cao nhưng vẫn trong giới hạn hoạt động của hầu hết card hiện nay. Tuy nhiên bạn nên kiểm tra quạt có quay đủ nhanh không, vệ sinh bụi trong thùng máy và cân nhắc giới hạn công suất (power limit) xuống khoảng 80% để giảm nhiệt mà tốc độ chỉ giảm vài phần trăm.
Bạn: Giới hạn công suất bằng cách nào vậy?
Trợ lý: Bạn có thể dùng lệnh nvidia-smi -pl 280 để đặt giới hạn 280 W, cần chạy với quyền quản trị. Giá trị này mất khi khởi động lại, nên nếu muốn giữ lâu dài hãy đặt nó trong một tác vụ chạy lúc khởi động.

Đánh giá sản phẩm: Tai nghe không dây (4/5 sao)

Âm thanh rõ, bass vừa đủ, pin dùng được khoảng 7 tiếng liên tục. Kết nối Bluetooth ổn định, chỉ thỉnh thoảng bị ngắt khi mình để điện thoại trong túi sau. Hộp sạc hơi to so với đối thủ. Giá 890.000 đồng thì mình thấy hợp lý. Sẽ giới thiệu cho bạn bè.

Toán: Một bể nước có thể tích 12 m³. Vòi thứ nhất chảy đầy bể trong 3 giờ, vòi thứ hai trong 6 giờ. Hỏi nếu mở cả hai vòi thì sau bao lâu bể đầy?
Giải: Trong một giờ, vòi thứ nhất chảy được 1/3 bể, vòi thứ hai chảy được 1/6 bể. Cả hai vòi chảy được 1/3 + 1/6 = 1/2 bể mỗi giờ. Vậy thời gian đầy bể là 1 : 1/2 = 2 giờ. Đáp số: 2 giờ.

Toán: Tìm số tự nhiên nhỏ nhất chia cho 3 dư 2, chia cho 5 dư 3 và chia cho 7 dư 2.
Giải: Số cần tìm có dạng n = 3a + 2. Chia 7 dư 2 và chia 3 dư 2 nên n − 2 chia hết cho 21, tức n = 21k + 2. Chia 5 dư 3: 21k + 2 ≡ k + 2 ≡ 3 (mod 5) nên k ≡ 1 (mod 5), chọn k = 1 được n = 23. Kiểm tra: 23 = 3 × 7 + 2, 23 = 5 × 4 + 3, 23 = 7 × 3 + 2. Đáp số: 23.

@dataclass
class Job:
    """A unit of work waiting in the queue."""

    job_id: str
    payload: dict
    attempts: int = 0
    tags: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)


class RetryQueue:
    """A small in-memory queue that retries failed jobs with backoff."""

    def __init__(self, max_attempts: int = 3, base_delay: float = 0.5) -> None:
        self._items: asyncio.Queue[Job] = asyncio.Queue()
        self.max_attempts = max_attempts
        self.base_delay = base_delay
        self.dead: list[Job] = []

    async def put(self, job: Job) -> None:
        await self._items.put(job)

    async def run(self, handler, workers: int = 4) -> None:
        async def worker(n: int) -> None:
            while True:
                job = await self._items.get()
                try:
                    await handler(job)
                except Exception as exc:  # noqa: BLE001 - we log and retry
                    job.attempts += 1
                    logger.warning("worker %d: job %s failed (%s)", n, job.job_id, exc)
                    if job.attempts >= self.max_attempts:
                        self.dead.append(job)
                    else:
                        # Exponential backoff: 0.5s, 1s, 2s, ...
                        await asyncio.sleep(self.base_delay * 2 ** (job.attempts - 1))
                        await self._items.put(job)
                finally:
                    self._items.task_done()

        tasks = [asyncio.create_task(worker(i)) for i in range(workers)]
        await self._items.join()
        for t in tasks:
            t.cancel()

def percentile(values: list[float], q: float) -> Optional[float]:
    """Return the q-th percentile (0..100) using linear interpolation."""
    if not values:
        return None
    ordered = sorted(values)
    pos = (len(ordered) - 1) * q / 100
    lo, hi = int(pos), min(int(pos) + 1, len(ordered) - 1)
    return ordered[lo] + (ordered[hi] - ordered[lo]) * (pos - lo)

function debounce<T extends (...args: any[]) => void>(fn: T, ms: number) {
  let timer: ReturnType<typeof setTimeout> | undefined;
  return (...args: Parameters<T>) => {
    if (timer) clearTimeout(timer);
    timer = setTimeout(() => fn(...args), ms);
  };
}

// result carries the outcome of one health check.
type result struct {
	url    string
	status int
	err    error
	took   time.Duration
}

func check(ctx context.Context, client *http.Client, url string) result {
	start := time.Now()
	req, err := http.NewRequestWithContext(ctx, http.MethodGet, url, nil)
	if err != nil {
		return result{url: url, err: err}
	}
	resp, err := client.Do(req)
	if err != nil {
		return result{url: url, err: err, took: time.Since(start)}
	}
	defer resp.Body.Close()
	return result{url: url, status: resp.StatusCode, took: time.Since(start)}
}

func main() {
	urls := []string{"https://example.org", "https://example.com/health", "http://localhost:8080/ping"}
	ctx, cancel := context.WithTimeout(context.Background(), 5*time.Second)
	defer cancel()

	client := &http.Client{Timeout: 3 * time.Second}
	results := make(chan result, len(urls))
	var wg sync.WaitGroup
	for _, u := range urls {
		wg.Add(1)
		go func(u string) {
			defer wg.Done()
			results <- check(ctx, client, u)
		}(u)
	}
	wg.Wait()
	close(results)

	for r := range results {
		if r.err != nil {
			fmt.Printf("%-35s ERROR %v\n", r.url, r.err)
			continue
		}
		fmt.Printf("%-35s %d (%v)\n", r.url, r.status, r.took.Round(time.Millisecond))
	}
}

#[derive(Debug, Clone, PartialEq)]
enum Token {
    Number(f64),
    Plus,
    Minus,
    Star,
    Slash,
    LParen,
    RParen,
}

#[derive(Debug)]
enum ParseError {
    UnexpectedChar(char, usize),
    UnexpectedEnd,
}

impl fmt::Display for ParseError {
    fn fmt(&self, f: &mut fmt::Formatter<'_>) -> fmt::Result {
        match self {
            ParseError::UnexpectedChar(c, i) => write!(f, "unexpected '{}' at {}", c, i),
            ParseError::UnexpectedEnd => write!(f, "unexpected end of input"),
        }
    }
}

fn tokenize(input: &str) -> Result<Vec<Token>, ParseError> {
    let chars: Vec<char> = input.chars().collect();
    let mut i = 0;
    let mut out = Vec::new();
    while i < chars.len() {
        let c = chars[i];
        match c {
            ' ' => { i += 1; }
            '+' => { out.push(Token::Plus); i += 1; }
            '-' => { out.push(Token::Minus); i += 1; }
            '*' => { out.push(Token::Star); i += 1; }
            '/' => { out.push(Token::Slash); i += 1; }
            '(' => { out.push(Token::LParen); i += 1; }
            ')' => { out.push(Token::RParen); i += 1; }
            '0'..='9' | '.' => {
                let start = i;
                while i < chars.len() && (chars[i].is_ascii_digit() || chars[i] == '.') {
                    i += 1;
                }
                let s: String = chars[start..i].iter().collect();
                out.push(Token::Number(s.parse().unwrap()));
            }
            other => return Err(ParseError::UnexpectedChar(other, i)),
        }
    }
    Ok(out)
}

typedef struct Node {

} Node;

static Node *insert(Node *root, int key) {
    if (root == NULL) {
        Node *n = malloc(sizeof(Node));
        if (!n) { perror("malloc"); exit(EXIT_FAILURE); }
        n->key = key;
        n->left = n->right = NULL;
        return n;
    }
    if (key < root->key) root->left = insert(root->left, key);
    else if (key > root->key) root->right = insert(root->right, key);
    return root;
}

    if (!root) return;
    inorder(root->left);
    printf("%d ", root->key);
    inorder(root->right);
}

            head_ = (head_ + 1) % data_.size();  // overwrite the oldest element
        }
    }

    std::vector<T> snapshot() const {
        std::vector<T> out;
        out.reserve(size_);
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < size_; ++i) out.push_back(data_[(head_ + i) % data_.size()]);
        return out;
    }

private:
    std::vector<T> data_;
    std::size_t head_, size_;
};

int main() {
    RingBuffer<std::string> log(3);
    for (auto s : {"a", "b", "c", "d", "e"}) log.push(s);
    for (const auto &line : log.snapshot()) std::cout << line << '\n';

class LRUCache:
    def __init__(self, capacity):
        self.capacity = capacity
        self.data = {}

    def get(self, key):
        if key not in self.data:
            return -1
        value = self.data.pop(key)
        self.data[key] = value  # move to the end: most recently used
        return value

    def put(self, key, value):
        if key in self.data:
            self.data.pop(key)
        elif len(self.data) >= self.capacity:
            oldest = next(iter(self.data))
            del self.data[oldest]
        self.data[key] = value

function mergeSort(arr) {
  if (arr.length <= 1) return arr;
  const mid = Math.floor(arr.length / 2);
  const left = mergeSort(arr.slice(0, mid));
  const right = mergeSort(arr.slice(mid));
  const out = [];
  let i = 0, j = 0;
  while (i < left.length && j < right.length) {
    out.push(left[i] <= right[j] ? left[i++] : right[j++]);
  }
  return out.concat(left.slice(i), right.slice(j));
}

"""A tiny command-line tool that summarizes a CSV file of measurements."""

from __future__ import annotations

import argparse
import csv
import statistics
import sys
from collections import defaultdict


def read_rows(path: str) -> list[dict[str, str]]:
    with open(path, newline="", encoding="utf-8") as fh:
        return list(csv.DictReader(fh))


def summarize(rows: list[dict[str, str]], key: str, value: str) -> dict[str, dict[str, float]]:
    groups: dict[str, list[float]] = defaultdict(list)
    for row in rows:
        try:
            groups[row[key]].append(float(row[value]))
        except (KeyError, ValueError):
            # Skip malformed rows rather than failing the whole run.
            continue
    return {
        name: {
            "count": len(vals),
            "mean": statistics.fmean(vals),
            "stdev": statistics.stdev(vals) if len(vals) > 1 else 0.0,
            "min": min(vals),
            "max": max(vals),
        }
        for name, vals in groups.items()
    }


def main(argv: list[str] | None = None) -> int:
    parser = argparse.ArgumentParser(description=__doc__)
    parser.add_argument("csv_path")
    parser.add_argument("--key", default="sensor")
    parser.add_argument("--value", default="reading")
    args = parser.parse_args(argv)

    rows = read_rows(args.csv_path)
    if not rows:
        print("no data", file=sys.stderr)
        return 1
    for name, stats in sorted(summarize(rows, args.key, args.value).items()):
        print(f"{name:<12} n={stats['count']:<4} mean={stats['mean']:.3f} sd={stats['stdev']:.3f}")
    return 0


if __name__ == "__main__":
    raise SystemExit(main())

import numpy as np


def softmax(x: np.ndarray, axis: int = -1) -> np.ndarray:
    x = x - x.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True)  # subtract the max for numerical stability
    e = np.exp(x)
    return e / e.sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True)


def attention(q: np.ndarray, k: np.ndarray, v: np.ndarray, mask: np.ndarray | None = None) -> np.ndarray:
    """Scaled dot-product attention. Shapes: q (T, d), k (S, d), v (S, d_v)."""
    scores = q @ k.T / np.sqrt(q.shape[-1])
    if mask is not None:
        scores = np.where(mask, scores, -1e9)
    return softmax(scores) @ v

// Store is a goroutine-safe key-value store with a simple read/write lock.
type Store struct {
	mu   sync.RWMutex
	data map[string][]byte
}

func New() *Store { return &Store{data: make(map[string][]byte)} }

func (s *Store) Get(key string) ([]byte, error) {
	s.mu.RLock()
	defer s.mu.RUnlock()
	v, ok := s.data[key]
	if !ok {
		return nil, ErrNotFound
	}
	out := make([]byte, len(v))
	copy(out, v) // return a copy so callers cannot mutate our state
	return out, nil
}

func (s *Store) Set(key string, value []byte) {
	s.mu.Lock()
	defer s.mu.Unlock()
	s.data[key] = append([]byte(nil), value...)
}

// Naive matrix multiply: C = A (m x k) * B (k x n), row-major.
void matmul(const std::vector<float> &A, const std::vector<float> &B,
            std::vector<float> &C, int m, int k, int n) {
    for (int i = 0; i < m; ++i) {
        for (int j = 0; j < n; ++j) {
            float acc = 0.0f;
            for (int p = 0; p < k; ++p) {
                acc += A[i * k + p] * B[p * n + j];
            }
            C[i * n + j] = acc;
        }
    }
}

// 4-bit quantization of a block of 32 floats with a shared scale.
struct Block4 { float scale; uint8_t q[16]; };

Block4 quantize_block(const float *x) {
    float amax = 0.0f;
    for (int i = 0; i < 32; ++i) amax = std::max(amax, std::abs(x[i]));
    Block4 b;
    b.scale = amax / 7.0f;
    float inv = b.scale != 0.0f ? 1.0f / b.scale : 0.0f;
    for (int i = 0; i < 16; ++i) {
        int lo = std::clamp((int)std::round(x[2 * i] * inv) + 8, 0, 15);
        int hi = std::clamp((int)std::round(x[2 * i + 1] * inv) + 8, 0, 15);
        b.q[i] = (uint8_t)(lo | (hi << 4));
    }
    return b;
}

Problem 1. A train leaves a station at 08:15 and travels at a constant speed of 84 km/h. A second train leaves the same station at 09:00 on a parallel track, traveling in the same direction at 112 km/h. At what time does the second train catch up with the first?

Solution. By 09:00 the first train has been traveling for 45 minutes, which is 0.75 hours, so it is ahead by 84 × 0.75 = 63 km. The second train gains 112 − 84 = 28 km every hour. The time needed to close a gap of 63 km is 63 / 28 = 2.25 hours, which is 2 hours and 15 minutes. Adding this to 09:00 gives 11:15. Check: at 11:15 the first train has traveled 84 × 3 = 252 km, and the second has traveled 112 × 2.25 = 252 km. The answer is 11:15.

User: Can you explain the difference between a process and a thread?
Assistant: Certainly. A process is an independent program in execution with its own address space, file handles and resources. A thread is a unit of execution inside a process; all threads of a process share the same memory. Because threads share memory, they are cheap to create and can communicate easily, but they also need careful synchronization to avoid race conditions. def retry(times=3, delay=0.2):
    def deco(fn):
        def wrapper(*args, **kwargs):
            last = None
            for attempt in range(times):
                try:
                    return fn(*args, **kwargs)
                except OSError as exc:
                    last = exc
                    time.sleep(delay * (attempt + 1))
            raise last
        return wrapper
    return deco
